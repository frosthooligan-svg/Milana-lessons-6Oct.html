04 · READING

The Race to the South Pole

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Antarctica remained one of the least explored places on Earth. Its extreme climate, powerful winds and vast areas of ice made exploration extremely dangerous. Nevertheless, several explorers were determined to reach the South Pole.

In 1911, two teams set out to achieve this ambitious goal. The British expedition was led by Robert Falcon Scott, while the Norwegian expedition was led by Roald Amundsen. Both men understood that careful preparation would be essential if they were to survive the journey.

Amundsen decided to use dogs to transport supplies across the ice. He had also studied the experience of Arctic communities and adapted their methods to Antarctic conditions. Scott, on the other hand, relied heavily on motorised transport and ponies, although these methods proved less effective than expected.

Amundsen's team reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911. Scott and his companions arrived several weeks later, only to discover that they had lost the race. Their return journey ended in tragedy, and none of the five members of Scott's polar party survived.

The expedition became a powerful example of how preparation, adaptability and knowledge can determine the outcome of an extremely challenging mission. Amundsen's success was not simply a result of courage. His ability to learn from others and adapt to harsh conditions played a crucial role.
